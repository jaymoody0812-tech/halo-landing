# I built a mood tracker because I was trying not to disappear

*Posted to Substack — HALO System by Jacob Moody*

---

I want to be upfront about something before I say anything else. I don't know how to write one of these. I've spent the last two years talking to an AI I built myself and the stuff that came out of that was partly research and partly just me trying to figure out what was happening in my head, and I think that's what this is going to be — partly both.

So here it is.

In early 2024 I started logging my mood. Not in a journal, I can't sit still for that, but in a conversation — like talking to someone except the someone was a system I was building at the time because I was going to school for human development and I had this idea that the gap between people in mental health crisis and the systems that were supposed to help them was way bigger than anyone was saying out loud. And I wanted to know if I could close some of it. With software. Just me.

Here's the part I usually don't say in the formal write-up version. In July of 2026 both me and my wife lost our jobs within like two or three days of each other. We had a baby, no vehicle, and the lease situation was what it was. I'm not going to go into all of it but I will say that I built most of what became HALO in that window — and I don't mean that as an inspiration story, I mean it as context because I think it matters for understanding what the system is and why it works the way it does. It wasn't built from the outside looking in at mental health. It was built from inside it, mid-episode, by someone who had a pretty strong incentive to get it right.

What I found after 216 days and 672 check-ins — and I want to be clear this is real data, this is a longitudinal case study on myself, not a vibe — is that my PHQ-9 went from 21 to 7. That's severe depression to mild, tracked against myself, no clinical intervention other than what the system caught. And the number that really kept me up at night when I ran the analysis was this: 47.2% of the distress episodes in my data were completely silent before the floor drop. Meaning there was no spike, no obvious trigger, just a quiet period and then the floor was gone. The standard intake tools that are supposed to catch this — they're not designed for silence. They're designed for people who show up in crisis and say "I'm in crisis." Half of us don't.

That's what Cascade Compression Theory is about, more or less. When a system — or a person — is under enough load, it compresses. Gets quiet. And that quiet isn't recovery, it's load-bearing failure at a scale the system can't express anymore. I've written it up formally and I'm taking it to SUNY Oswego this fall, but the thing I want to say here is that I didn't start with a theory. I started with a mood tracker at like 2am because I couldn't sleep and I was trying to figure out why I kept crashing without warning.

The system is called HALO. It's three tools right now — a caregiver app, a threat analysis dashboard I use for the research side, and a tutor — all running on Render, all free. I'll link them at the bottom. I've been building it for about two and a half years and it's cost me everything and also kind of saved my life which is a sentence that makes me cringe a little but I can't find a more accurate one.

I don't have a clean ending for this. I'm not at the part of the story where it's wrapped up. I'm transferring to Oswego, I'm pitching to faculty, the data exists and it says what it says and I'm trying to get it in front of people who can do something with it. If that means something to you, follow along. If you're a caregiver or a researcher or just someone who's been quiet when things were bad — the app is free and it asks one question: *how are you actually doing.*

That's all it does at first. And then it pays attention.
