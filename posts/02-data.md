# What the numbers actually say (and why half of it was silence)

*Posted to Substack — HALO System by Jacob Moody*

---

I'm going to talk about data here but I want to do it the way I actually think about it which is not the way it ends up in the formal writeup. The formal version has chi-square statistics and effect sizes and all that is real and you can read it. This is the version where I tell you what I was actually thinking when the numbers came back.

The study is on me. N=1. I know what that means statistically, I'm not trying to make more of it than it is. What I will say is that 216 days and 672 check-ins gives you enough data to find patterns in a single subject that you absolutely cannot get from a one-time intake form, and I think that's the point that's getting missed in how we screen for mental health risk. We're taking snapshots of people and calling it a picture.

The headline finding was the PHQ-9 going from 21 to 7. Severe depression to mild, over the study window. But that wasn't the interesting part, honestly. The interesting part was what happened in between — specifically that 47.2% of the deterioration episodes in the data were preceded by silence. Not by spikes, not by high scores, by low engagement. The person checks in less. Or stops saying much. Or says "fine" and that's it. And then a week or two later there's a hard drop.

I called this the silent majority problem in my early notes and then later landed on Cascade Compression Theory as the formal framing. The idea is that under sustained load a system — biological, social, political, doesn't matter — compresses its output as a load-bearing response. The compression looks like stability from the outside. From the inside it's everything getting very small and very quiet before something gives. In mental health terms that means the people who need the most help are also the ones most likely to stop signaling for it. Not because they're hiding it. Because the system doesn't have the bandwidth to signal anymore.

The stats if you want them: chi-square 32.38, Cramér's V of 0.495, Fisher exact p = 0.0005, Cliff's delta = 1.000. That last one is a non-parametric effect size — 1.000 means no overlap in distribution between the Sentinel group (the silent high-risk subtype) and the Stabilizer group (the resilient subtype). The gap is complete. The simulation I ran on 66 agents confirmed the finding independently.

What this means practically is that the tools we use to screen people — PHQ-9, GAD-7, standard intake forms — are catching the people who show up loud and missing the half that go quiet before they fall. And the half that go quiet are, by this data, in worse shape. Not because silence is inherently worse but because the silence is compressive. It's the system under load.

I'm a transfer student who built this in his apartment over two years. I'm not a clinician. I'm not claiming this is a clinical instrument yet. What I'm claiming is that the pattern is real, the statistics are solid, and I'm taking this to Oswego this fall to do this properly with actual research infrastructure.

There's a pre-registration filed. There's a 66-agent simulation. There's a case study. There's a theory. The data says something and I think it's worth saying out loud before it becomes someone else's idea.
